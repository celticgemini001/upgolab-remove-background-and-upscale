Cutout and Upscale in One Workflow: Where the Edges Fail

Unlike denoise order, the order of cutout and upscale genuinely matters — and one direction is unrecoverable. A matte computed at low resolution produces edges that upscaling widens into a visible halo, and no later step can narrow them again.

14 November 2026 · edge-transition width measured across the two orders

Background removal and upscaling look like two independent operations on the same file, so most people run them in whatever order the tool makes convenient. They are not independent. The matte is a per-pixel alpha decision, and upscaling is a per-pixel interpolation — and interpolation applied to an alpha channel produces exactly the artifact that compositing is most sensitive to.

The asymmetry that decides the order

Consider the two orders on a 500×500 product photo with a clean subject boundary, targeting 1000×1000:

  • Cut out, then upscale. The matte is computed at 500×500, so its edge is one source pixel wide — then the upscale widens it to two target pixels while softening it. A 1px hard alpha edge becomes a 2px gradient.
  • Upscale, then cut out. The upscale softens the subject boundary itself (same interpolation, same effect on the RGB), and then the matte is computed at 1000×1000 — so its edge is one target pixel wide, sharper in relative terms.

In both cases something is 1–2px soft. The difference is which thing. Upscaling second means the softness lands on the alpha channel, where it composites as a fringe. Upscaling first means the softness lands on the RGB edge, where it is partially masked by the subject's own colour.

Rule: upscale first, then matte — when the source is the constraint. A matte computed at higher resolution has more pixels to decide with, and its edge is narrower relative to the image. This is the opposite of the "do all destructive steps last" instinct, because a matte is not destructive in the same way — it is a decision, and later decisions benefit from more information.

When the reverse order wins

If the matte is already perfect and you want to preserve it exactly — a hand-drawn mask, a rotoscoped sequence, a mask you spent an hour on — do not recompute it after upscaling. Run the matte, then upscale the composite, and accept the 2px soft edge. Recomputing risks a different result on the enlarged version, and consistency matters more than 1px of edge width.

So the decision is: is the matte cheap or expensive? Cheap (auto-detected) → upscale first, recompute at high resolution. Expensive (manual) → matte first, upscale the composite, keep the mask.

Why the halo appears at all

A matte edge is a transition from alpha 1.0 to alpha 0.0. If it transitions over 2px instead of 1px, the composite blends the subject with the new background over 2px. On a subject against a colour that differs strongly from the subject's edge colour, that transition band reads as a glow — the classic "cut out badly" look.

There is a second, subtler cause that upscaling makes worse: colour bleed. If the original background was green and the subject is a person, the pixels right at the subject boundary contain a mix of green and skin. A matte will correctly call those pixels partially transparent, but the RGB values still carry green. Upscaling spreads that green further into the subject. The result is a faint green rim that survives the cutout, because the cutout only decides transparency, not colour.

The fix for bleed is a separate step — spill suppression — and the order is: suppress spill before upscaling, because upscaling multiplies the contaminated pixels and gives you more to clean up.

The order that respects all of it

  1. Spill suppression on the source. Removes background colour contamination at the boundary pixels. Cheap, must happen early.
  2. Upscale. At 2× if possible; the detail measurements say 2× retains ~21% of high-frequency energy versus ~1.3% at 4×.
  3. Matte at final resolution. More pixels to decide with, narrower relative edge.
  4. Composite. Nothing after this should touch the alpha channel.

Note that this is a different order than the denoise case, where the sequence barely mattered (0.8% difference in comb residual). The difference is that denoise and deinterlace both operate on the same domain — the RGB pixels — while cutout creates a second channel whose behaviour under interpolation is different. Cross-domain operations are where order becomes non-negotiable.

The cheap check

Composite the result on a saturated background (magenta is the classic choice) and look at the boundary at 100%. Any band of background colour between subject and new background is your edge width. If it is more than 2px at the final resolution, something in the chain softened it and you can find which step by comparing before/after each one.

Magenta is used because it appears nowhere in natural images — any magenta in the result is unambiguously a matte failure, not image content. That is why the old compositing tutorials insist on it.

Reproducing it

# order A: matte at source resolution, then upscale the RGBA
rgba_small = cutout(src)                  # 500x500
out_a = rgba_small.resize((1000,1000), Image.LANCZOS)

# order B: upscale RGB, then matte at target resolution
rgb_big = src.resize((1000,1000), Image.LANCZOS)
out_b = cutout(rgb_big)                   # 1000x1000

# measure: width of the alpha transition band in each
alpha = np.asarray(out.convert("A"), float) / 255
band = ((alpha > 0.05) & (alpha < 0.95)).sum(axis=0).max()
